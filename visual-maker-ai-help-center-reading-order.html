Which Help Center Guide Should You Read First? A Short Route Through Visual Maker AI's Docs

Documentation gets ignored because it is hard to know where to start. The Visual Maker AI Help Center is small enough to read in an evening: four guides, each one aimed at a different question. This is a suggested order, based on what each guide says, so you spend your first session creating instead of hunting.

The four guides at a glance

The Help Center lists these:

  1. Getting Started with Visual Maker AI – how to create your first image or video in under five minutes.
  2. Writing Effective AI Image Prompts – a practical guide to prompts that produce better images.
  3. Understanding Credits and Pricing – how generation credits are charged, how subscription and one-time credits differ, and where to find current plans.
  4. Turning Images into Video – how to animate a still image or generate short clips.

Every guide ends with "next steps" and "more guides" links, so you can move between them without returning to the index.

Route one: you want a picture today

Start with Getting Started, then jump straight to the prompt guide. The prompt guide is the most reusable of the four because it gives you a structure rather than a feature tour. It breaks a prompt into four layers: subject, style, lighting and atmosphere, and composition. Its before-and-after example makes the point quickly: "a coffee mug" is vague, while a matte black ceramic mug on a weathered oak table with steam, soft window light from the left and a 50mm lens gives the model something to work with.

The same guide has quick-reference tables of style keywords (photorealistic, illustration, 3D render, painting, editorial) and lighting keywords (bright and airy, dramatic, golden hour, studio). It also lists common mistakes: mixing conflicting styles such as watercolor and photorealistic, forgetting the aspect ratio, and overloading the prompt with detail. Its advice is to prioritise the three or four most important elements.

Route two: you want to animate something

Read the video guide after you have a still image you like. It describes two modes: Image to Video, where you upload a still and animate it, and Text to Video, where you describe the whole scene. The steps are short: open the Generate page, choose the Video tab, set the input mode, upload or drag in an image, optionally add a motion prompt, and generate.

The tips are worth keeping:

  • Use a sharp source. The video can only be as sharp as the image; the guide suggests 1024px or larger.
  • Simplify the scene. A clear focal point animates better than a cluttered one.
  • Be specific about motion. "Subtle breathing" and "gentle wind" give more natural results than "make it move."

It is also honest about limits: video costs more than images (typically 10 credits per clip, according to the guide), clips are a few seconds long, busy scenes can produce artifacts, and output arrives as MP4 files. Generation typically takes 15 to 60 seconds.

Route three: you want to know what it costs first

Read the credits guide before generating anything large. It explains how credits are charged and the difference between subscription credits and one-time credits, and it points to the current plans rather than quoting a price that could go stale. If you plan to make a lot of video, this is the guide that stops a surprise.

A sensible first evening

If I had to compress it, the order is: Getting Started, prompts, credits, video. That sequence follows how most sessions actually go: you make an image, you improve the prompt, you check what you are spending, and then you move the best image into motion.

One more place to look

The Help Center links the Prompt Library for ready-made prompts, and the main text to image page is where the prompt guide's advice gets put to work. Read a guide, try one thing, and come back to the next one only when you hit a question it answers.
