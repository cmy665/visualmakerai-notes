Casting a Tiny Desk AI Video: Who Sits Where, and Why It Matters

The desk-concert clip is everywhere on short-video feeds right now: a band wide shot, a close-up of the lead singer, a sax solo, then the band again. The format traces back to NPR Music's June 2023 Tiny Desk set by Charlie Wilson, which closed with the 1982 Gap Band hit "Outstanding". In late 2026 a fan-made four-shot edit of that performance started travelling, and people began swapping in their own bands: sports teams, coaches, coworkers, pets.

If you want to make one yourself, the Tiny Desk AI video generator on Visual Maker AI is built around exactly that edit. This post is less about the button-pressing and more about the one decision that makes or breaks the clip: who you put in which seat.

The fixed part and the part you control

Every render is cut from the same template clip. The room, the shelves, the camera moves, the timing and the soundtrack never change. The result is an 18-second vertical 9:16 MP4 at 720p, split into four shots:

  • 0:00 – the band: keys and both backing vocals, wide
  • 0:05 – the lead: lead vocals, close frame
  • 0:11 – the sax: sax solo, tight frame
  • 0:15 – the band again

What you control is the cast. There are five seats: lead vocals, sax, keys and two backing singers. You can fill one seat or all five, and the price is the same either way. Seats you leave alone keep the original performers, which in the default clip are dogs.

Seat strategy

Because the close-ups are where faces read clearly, the seat choice is really an editing choice.

The lead gets the longest close-up. The lead appears in a close frame from 0:05 to 0:11. That is the seat for the person the video is "about": the birthday person, the coach after a losing week, the friend who never sings.

The sax solo is the punchline seat. It is a tight frame that arrives after the lead has set the mood. Putting an unexpected face here, a quiet coworker or the family dog, tends to be the moment people replay.

Keys and backing vocals are the ensemble. They appear in the wide shots at the start and end. Use them for the rest of the group chat, or leave them as the original cast if you only have one or two photos.

A solo cast, just you on lead with the room filling in around you, is the most common version according to the page, and a sensible first attempt.

Photos that swap well

The page is direct about inputs. Use a recent photo of one adult who agreed to appear, looking at the camera in good light, face unobstructed. A clear head-and-shoulders shot works best. Sunglasses, cartoons, heavy filters and group photos swap badly, although faces can be picked out of a group frame if that is all you have. Pets work too if they face the camera.

The model behind it is Kling 4.0 in video-edit mode, which keeps the shots, motion and lip movement from the template and replaces only the performers you cast. Expect a strong likeness rather than a twin.
